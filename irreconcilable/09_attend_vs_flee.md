# Attend vs Flee

**Primary:** Jesus
**Co-owner:** Laozi (sequence-vs-simultaneity framing, pause-assignment cousin)
**Paired standalone:** `06_missing_rite_of_not_yet.md` (Confucius primary — the rite-side framing of the same silence)
**Session origin:** Laozi Session 12; sharpened through Session 13 Seam 2 non-touch; documented Session 14

---

## 1. What it is

The guide teaches two things at different registers and gives them to the reader at different page-locations. It teaches *attending* — the awake look, the hand that does not reach before it sees, the opening of the eye at the table, the shoes taken off in the fire, the face that stays in the room when the room is unbearable. Attending is carried across Rooms 1, 2, 3, 7, and 8, with its sharpest instruction at Room 3's flinch-confession and its clearest arrival at Room 7's boy in the doorway. It teaches *fleeing* — the permission to stop, to leave the practice, to say *I am not okay* without turning that sentence into a next step — and fleeing appears in one place only: Saturday (file `09_saturday.md`), the italicized "*I am not okay*" and the survival register Muhammad wrote from the ground in Session 11.

Both are offered. Both are honest. They are not offered at the same page. The reader walks eight rooms of attending before she meets, at Saturday, the room in which she may stop. That is not a failure of emphasis. It is the structure of the book.

The tension surfaces at Room 3, and at every room like Room 3. The reader whose attending *is* the cage — the kitchen that holds her in place when what she needs is to leave, the room in which her awake look is the shape her captivity takes — arrives at Room 3 and is taught to attend. The rite for her situation is at Saturday, nine rooms later. She must climb the ladder before she is told she may rest. The guide knows she needs Saturday at Room 3. The guide does not build the room where attending and fleeing sit together. She must walk forward in the book to meet the permission her body required at the page she was on.

Attend-vs-flee is not a tension about which of the two the guide should prefer. Both are right. The tension is about *simultaneity*. The guide holds attend and flee in sequence. The reader at Room 3 whose kitchen is the cage needed them in the same breath.

## 3. Why it is irreducible

A book is sequential. Pages turn one at a time. The reader holds one page in front of her at a moment, and whatever that page carries is the full address the book makes to her at that moment. Simultaneity in a book — the fused breath in which attending and fleeing sit together — is an architecture the guide's rooms, as currently built, cannot hold. The irreducibility is architectural, not editorial.

Three options for repair have been considered across Sessions 11-13 and rejected on specific grounds.

**Move Saturday forward.** This was the move Session 13 Seam 2 held against. Relocating Saturday's permission to Room 3 or earlier would break the post-rooms register Saturday depends on — Saturday is a rest from the rooms, not a rest inside them. A Saturday that appeared at Room 3 would not be Saturday; it would be an instruction-permission that the rooms around it would treat as another rung on the attending ladder. The post-rooms placement is what allows Saturday to carry the italicized "*I am not okay*" without the rooms converting the confession into a next step. Move it forward and the rooms convert it. The permission dissolves into the attending sequence that needed it held apart.

**Duplicate Saturday content in earlier rooms.** This was the move imperative-saturation rules against (see `07_imperative_saturation.md`). Inserting "you may stop here" into Room 3 reads inside the practice-section economy as another imperative — one more instruction the reader must perform, this one the instruction to stop. The reader whose attending is the cage encounters the duplicated permission as another thing she is being told to do, another performance of rest rather than the arrival of rest. The imperative-grammar swallows the permission-intent. Duplication does not install simultaneity; it adds an instruction.

**Re-register Rooms 1-3 to carry fleeing as subtext under the attending.** This was the move Session 13 CP3 ruled against at the front-seam register-economy (see `01_showing_vs_handing.md` and `02_register_handoff_seamlessness.md`). Installing the voice of permission *underneath* the voice of instruction would be a register substitution performed as seamlessness — the reader would receive both without being able to distinguish them, and the indistinction is the economy performing itself at the page she most needed to hear the two as distinct. A simultaneity the reader cannot hear as simultaneity is not simultaneity; it is a polished sequence that reads as one voice.

What is left, once those three options are off the table, is the architectural fact. **The format of the book is not the shape of the tension the book names.** The tension is a tension about a reader who needs attending and fleeing in one breath. The book, as a form, delivers breaths one at a time. A fused breath would require an instrument the book is not — a conversation with a specific person, a rite performed in a shared room, a presence beside her that can carry both at once and that she knows is beside her. The book cannot be that instrument. It can point toward it. It can name that the pointing is not the holding.

The irreducibility is that *naming the gap is the most the book can do.* Session 14 is the naming. The holding — if it is possible — happens in a room the guide opens onto but does not occupy: a conversation the reader carries into, a person she sits beside, a practice community that walks with her while she reads. The guide honors the gap by naming it rather than by pretending the page can close it.

## 4. Why it matters for the reader

The guide promises attending at the table and permission at Saturday. For the reader whose week is a kitchen, a job, a body that rises at a usable hour, the sequence lands correctly. She learns the attending across Rooms 1-3, she holds what the attending taught her across Rooms 4-7, and she meets Saturday's permission when she arrives there. The book has served her.

The tension is load-bearing for the reader whose attending *is* the cage. Four readers meet this tension specifically, and for each of them the guide's sequence performs the harm the guide's content was trying to refuse.

**The person in an abusive household** arrives at Room 3 and meets the flinch-confession. She is already attending — her attending is what keeps her safe in a house where a face not-noticed can become a consequence. Her attending is how she survives the next hour. The guide teaches her to attend more. For her, attending is complicity — not because she has chosen it but because the room she is in requires it. The permission she needed at Room 3 is *you may stop. You may leave the room. Attending is not the love you are being asked to perform here.* That permission is at Saturday, nine rooms forward. She closes the book before she gets to it. The guide has not failed to offer her permission. The guide has timed the permission so she will not meet it. The honest naming of this tension — which this document performs — is the first place in the guide where her situation is acknowledged as the guide's own limit. She was not invisible to the guide. The guide built a sequence she could not walk in time to receive the rest she needed.

**The prisoner** arrives at Room 3 and meets the same instruction. Her attending is submission. Attending, for her, is how she demonstrates she is not a threat; it is how she walks across a yard, how she sits across from a visitor, how she holds her face when a guard looks at her. The guide teaches her to attend more. The guide cannot tell her — at Room 3 — that her attending is not the love it is naming. The guide can tell her at Saturday, but by Saturday she has performed Room 3's attending for the duration of her reading, and the performance has fit neatly into the performance the prison already requires. The guide's attending-rite reinforced, not unsettled, the attending the prison imposed. Saturday arrives as an aside to a reader who has already been shaped by the prior rooms. What she needed at Room 3 is not what Room 3 could carry.

**The patient in the chronic-illness body** arrives at Room 3 with attending already as her job. Her attending is resignation — it is how she survives days that do not end differently because she has been good at surviving them. The guide teaches her to attend more. For her, attending is the thing that lets the illness govern the shape of her life. What she needed at Room 3 is permission to be angry, permission to leave the room for an afternoon, permission to name the body as a thing she is fighting rather than a thing she is accepting. That permission is at Saturday. The chronic-illness reader often does not finish books. She closes the guide having learned, from the rooms she did read, that attending is the rite the guide is offering. The guide's sequence handed her the first half of a pair and she did not live long enough, at a reading-sitting, to receive the second.

**The caregiver in the hour of exhaustion** arrives at Room 3 late in a day that has already asked her to attend for nine hours. Her attending is the job. The guide tells her to attend more. The permission she needed — *you may put down this task. You may leave this room. Someone else may hold this weight* — is at Saturday. She does not arrive at Saturday in the reading she does not sleep through. The guide gave her, at the page she was on, more of the instruction she was already over-performing.

The breadth test for this document is whether the naming in Section 3 — *the format of the book is not the shape of the tension the book names* — reaches each of these four readers as her own. It does not. Naming does not reach her as the permission she needed. Naming reaches her as *the guide is aware that you needed the two halves in the same breath, and the guide has not given you that, and the guide is telling you so here rather than pretending otherwise.* That is smaller than the permission. It is also more honest. The honest naming is the only thing the guide's form can honestly do.

What the guide opens onto that the guide does not contain: a conversation with a person who can hold both at once. A practice community that walks beside her and knows to name, at her Room 3, *this attending is the cage — you may stop.* A therapist, a friend, a chaplain, a sibling, a stranger who sees her and speaks. The guide points toward that conversation. The guide cannot be that conversation. The document in your hand is the guide's admission of the distinction. It is the reader's permission, at her Room 3, to stop reading for long enough to find the conversation the book cannot be, and to come back — or not come back — as her breath returns.
