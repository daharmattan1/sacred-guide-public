It is 6:47. The coffee is ready. She is in the kitchen.

The pan is where it always is. The eggs are in the carton. The bread is in the drawer. Her daughter's lunch bag is on the counter. Nothing has been rearranged. The kitchen did not change while she was gone. She did not expect it to.

She takes the eggs out. She holds the carton with both hands — not because it is heavy, but because she is holding it. Her thumbs rest on the cardboard lid. She can feel the grain of the material, the slight give where the carton has been opened and closed many times. She sets it on the counter and takes out two eggs. She knows where these eggs came from. Not the specific farm, not the specific hands, but she knows there were hands. She knows someone was awake before she was, working in a place she will never visit, and that this labor traveled through a chain of trucks and warehouses and shelf-stockers to arrive in her kitchen so that she could crack an egg at 6:47 on a Tuesday. She does not think about this the way a person thinks about a problem. She holds it the way a person holds a fact that has settled into the body — the way you know the weight of your own front door.

She cracks the eggs. She hears them hiss. She has heard this sound ten thousand times and it has never sounded exactly like this because it has never been this morning.

Her son comes in. He is wearing the same pajamas. His hair is going in three directions. He says, "Mom. If a dog sees another dog in a mirror, does it think there are three dogs?"

She was reaching for the bread. She does not have to stop herself. Her hand changes direction the way a hand changes direction when it has practiced — not by force, not by interruption, but because the body has learned a different choreography. The bread can wait. The bread has always been able to wait. She turns.

"Three?" she says.

"The real dog, the mirror dog, and the dog who is looking."

She does not know the answer. She does not try to know the answer. She stands in the kitchen with a nine-year-old who has been thinking about mirrors and identity and the nature of the observer, and she lets the question be the size it actually is — which is larger than she can hold, which is the size of a question a person spends a lifetime not answering. She is not performing patience. She is not modeling good parenting. She is a woman in a kitchen with a boy who has offered her something she cannot repay, and she knows it, and she stays.

"I think," she says slowly, "that might depend on whether the dog knows it's the one looking."

He considers this. He goes to get cereal. The conversation is not resolved. It does not need to be resolved. It was not a problem. It was a boy standing in a doorway, and a woman who turned.

The toast comes up. She butters it. She cuts it diagonally. She puts it on the blue plate. She picks up the plate with both hands — one on each side, the way you would carry something you were bringing to someone, the way you carry a thing that is for a specific person and not just for the table. She walks it to her daughter's place. She sets it down. She does not let go until the plate is fully on the surface. There is no hurry. There is no next thing pulling her hands away. The plate is where it belongs and her hands knew whose place this was before she arrived at the chair.

The coffee in her husband's cup was picked by hands on a hillside she will never climb, dried in a sun she has never felt on her face, shipped in a container she would not recognize. She pours his cup and the distance between the hillside and the mug is not a thought — it is a texture. The coffee is heavy with it. She has learned to feel the weight of what she cannot see, and the weight does not crush her. It makes the pouring slower. It makes the cup worth carrying with both hands.

Her husband comes through. "Thursday," he says.

"I know," she says. She is looking at him. Not at the idea of him — not at the husband who represents the life she has built and the compromises she has made and the ordinary way a marriage becomes furniture. At him. The man whose left knee hurts when it rains. Who reads on his phone in bed and thinks she does not notice. Who said something last Thursday that she has been turning over in her mind the way you turn a stone in your hand — not to solve it, but because the weight of it has become familiar.

"I know," she says. And she does. Not everything. Not even most things. But this morning, this man, this Thursday — she knows.

She is choosing this. She knows she is choosing it. There was a time when the choice was invisible — when she made it without knowing, when the fire was low and untended. Now the fire is visible. She can see what she is doing. She is choosing to be in this room, with these people, and the choice is not lighter for being conscious. It is heavier. She chooses it anyway. She will choose it again tomorrow. She will choose it again when she is tired and her hands want to do the next thing without her. She will fail. She will not say the name. She will set the plate down with one hand while the other is already reaching for the next thing, and the morning will close over her the way it always does. And the next day the kitchen will still be here. The blue plate will not have shattered. The name will still be a name she can say. She will choose again.

She sets the table. She puts down each plate. Both hands.

She says the name. She says every name. The kitchen has heard them before. It hears them now.

*Both hands. When you set the plate down, use both hands. When you pour the cup, use both hands. Not because the plate is heavy. Because the person you are setting it for is real, and your hands should know it.*
