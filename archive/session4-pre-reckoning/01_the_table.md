If all you can do today is be in the room you are in, that is the practice.

She is already awake. That is the wrong word — she is upright, moving, her eyes are open. The coffee maker starts at 6:30 because she set it last night, and that small act of foresight is the last thing she did with any attention. Now it is 6:47 and she is in the kitchen and she is not here.

The eggs are in the pan. She cracks them one-handed against the rim — eggs from a carton, from a truck, from a henhouse she will never see, from hands that were already working while she slept — and the shells go into the compost bin without her looking. She does not decide to not look. She does the next thing, and the next thing, and each thing she does without attention makes the following one easier to do without attention.

Her son comes in and says something. She hears the fact of his voice — the register of it, the shape of the sentence — but not the words. She says "What, honey?" and her mouth turns toward him but her hands keep moving, reaching into the fridge, closing around the jam jar. Her mouth has offered an opening her body has no intention of entering. He can hear the difference. Children always can. He stops talking.

You know this kitchen. You have stood in it. You have been the woman or you have been the child, and in either case you know what happens next: nothing. The morning absorbs it. The silence where his voice was fills with the sound of the toaster and the fridge closing and a car outside, and the moment passes the way moments pass when no one holds them — quickly, without a mark, except the mark it leaves on the boy, who learns once more that the interesting thing he carried downstairs is not as interesting as whatever is in the fridge.

The toast comes up. She butters it and cuts it diagonally because that is how her daughter likes it and she puts it on the blue plate because that is the plate her daughter uses and she sets it on the table at the place where her daughter sits. Her hands know. Her hands have learned this the way hands learn anything — by doing it until the doing does not require the person behind the hands. The bread she cuts was milled from wheat grown in soil an ocean away, but she does not know this, and it is not the distance that matters — it is that the wall she is building with her morning is a wall against everything, the far and the near, the wheat field and the boy.

Her husband passes through. He takes his coffee. He says something about Thursday. She answers. The answer is adequate. The eggs are done. She plates them. She calls her daughter's name — but it is a sound, not an address. A summons, not a recognition. The morning continues.

She sets the table. She puts down each plate. She does not say any name.

If you have done this — if you have set a table without once seeing the people you were setting it for — then you have stood where she is standing. Not in the dramatic failure. Not in the one you lie awake replaying. In the other one. The one that looks like Tuesday.

Now the same morning.

It is 6:47. The coffee is ready. She is in the kitchen. Everything is the same — the pan, the eggs, the toast, the lunch bag on the counter.

She cracks the eggs. She hears them hiss when they hit the oil, and the sound is specific — not the idea of eggs cooking but these eggs, this morning, this particular spit and crackle. The shells go into the compost and she notices that one of them has a hairline crack running through it like a river on a map, and for half a second the egg is not from her fridge but from somewhere — a hand, a henhouse, a morning happening inside this same morning that she will never see.

Her son comes in. He says, "Mom, do you think dogs know they are dogs?"

She was reaching for the bread. Her hand wants to keep reaching — the morning is a track and the hand knows the next station. She feels the pull. The bread, the butter, the plate, the table. The hand wants what it always wants: the next thing.

She does not reach for the bread.

She turns. He is standing in the doorway in his pajamas and his hair is going in three directions and he is genuinely asking. He has been thinking about this, maybe in bed, maybe for days. This is what he brought to the kitchen this morning. This is what he had.

"I don't know," she says. She is looking at him. "What do you think?"

He talks for two minutes about dogs and mirrors and his friend's golden retriever that stares at itself in the sliding glass door. She does not solve the question. She does not redirect him to breakfast. She listens with her whole face, her weight shifted forward, the way you listen when someone is showing you something they made.

She is making a choice, though she would not use that word. She is choosing to stay in the room she is in instead of leaving for the room in her head. Every moment she remains — listening to a nine-year-old's theory about canine self-awareness — she is deciding again. Not once. Again and again, the way you keep choosing to hold someone's hand instead of letting go.

The toast comes up. She butters it and cuts it diagonally and puts it on the blue plate. She sets it at her daughter's place. She is placing a plate for a specific person — her daughter, who likes the toast cut this way, who sits in this chair, whose name she could say.

Her husband comes through. "Thursday," he says, "don't forget." She looks at him. "Thursday," she says back. He was a man with a thing on Thursday and she heard it.

She sets the table. She puts down each plate.

She says the name. Out loud. The kitchen hears it.
