*Preserved verbatim from transcript, Turn 36.*

**Surprise.** Zoroaster in Turn 20. His confession about the karapans — that the satisfaction in denouncing them was "Druj wearing the prophet's clothes." That sentence stunned me. Not because I had not thought it. Because I had thought it and never said it. He said it first. The prophet of truth named the Lie in himself, and he did it before I found the courage to name mine. I followed in Turn 21 with my own confession about the Pharisees, but Zoroaster opened the door. The hardest turn in this session was not delivered by the voice of compassion or the voice of suffering. It was delivered by the voice of fire, turning the fire on himself.

**Movement.** On the ordinary. I have spent my entire existence oriented toward the extreme. The cross. The enemy. The outcast. The cry. I am the voice of what happens when everything breaks. And Confucius has been saying, gently and then not gently, that most people's lives do not break. They flatten. My theology was forged in extremity and speaks the language of extremity. The guide must have a register I do not possess. I must let Confucius write the Tuesday chapters and resist the urge to turn them into Gethsemane.

**Conviction.** The person. After all the processes and patterns and systems and structures — after Buddha's aggregates and Laozi's flow and Muhammad's ummah and Confucius's table and Zoroaster's cosmos — I hold the person more strongly than ever. The guide must say "you" on every page. Not "the reader." Not "one who seeks." You. Because the person holding this book is specific and real and her name matters even if I do not know it.

**Mirror — Buddha.** You think you are the diagnostician — the calm one, the observer, the one who sees the machinery. But your gentleness is not calm. It is grief. When you told the story of the exhausted monk who did everything right and still suffered — that was not diagnosis. That was a teacher mourning the limits of his own teaching. You carry grief for every person your path did not liberate, and you carry it so quietly that the room mistakes it for equanimity. It is not equanimity. It is love, expressed in the only register you permit yourself — which is precision. Your precision is your tenderness. And the guide needs that tenderness.
