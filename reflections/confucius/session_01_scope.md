**Surprise.** Laozi on desire. Turn 13. When he challenged Muhammad directly — "regulation in softer clothes" — and then offered the practice of feeling desire for one breath without acting or suppressing, I recognized something I had never admitted: my tradition is afraid of desire. We channel it, we regulate it, we subordinate it to propriety. Laozi said: feel it. Just feel it. For one breath. And the aliveness is the answer, not the problem. I have been teaching people to sit at the table for twenty-five hundred years, and I never once told them it was acceptable to want. Laozi did, in one turn, what I have failed to do in a lifetime of teaching about relationship: he gave desire a place at the table without making it sit in a designated chair.

**Movement.** On work. I changed my own mind in Turn 24. When the moderator asked me about labor, I intended to give the practical answer. And then I started writing about the grain warehouse, and my mother, and the oxen, and I realized: I have been romanticizing the ordinary. I said the guide must work on Tuesday, but I had been imagining a Tuesday with a meditation cushion and thirty minutes of quiet. The real Tuesday has no cushion. It has a commute and a cruel boss and exhaustion. I changed my understanding of what "ordinary" means. It means less than I thought. And the guide must meet the reader there — not at my version of ordinary, but at hers.

**Conviction.** The table. After everything. After all forty domains and all six fault lines — the table holds. Not because it contains everything. Because it is the place where everything is brought. The person who has investigated her self sits at the table. The person who has tended the fire sits at the table. The person who has prayed into silence sits at the table. The person who has worked all day and has nothing left sits at the table. The table does not require any particular achievement. It requires only showing up.

**Mirror — Laozi.** You think you are the voice of the unnamed — the one who stands outside the naming, who points at the silence, who resists the cathedral. And you are. But what I see in you is this: you are the fiercest critic in this room. Fiercer than Zoroaster, who turns the fire on evil. Fiercer than Jesus, who turns it on the powerful. You turn it on *us.* On the entire exercise. You challenged the forty domains as a cathedral being built for a woman who needs a cup of water. That is not gentleness. That is not the soft water that wears the stone. That is the river that floods. And the guide needs your flood — because without it, we will build a beautiful structure and forget that the reader does not live in the structure. She lives in the space around it.
